# Privacy Risks from Machine-Learning Training Data

Because machine learning models need to learn from data from a wide range of sources, it is important that the model doesn’t overfit and therefore, memorized training data. Sometimes this is done intentionally by models, so they will be more robust. For example, it makes sense to memorize all the US presidents because that's something all the school children in the US will be asking about. However, memorizing data isn’t a good idea in terms of privacy. For example, a malicious user may be able to make the model reproduce private details such as names, contact info, medical information, or confidential business records, just to name a few. Getting the AI to admit confidential or guarded information isn’t even a hard thing to do. Speaking from personal experience, I love the game Gandalf AI—where you try to get a series of progressively more secure AIs to admit a secret key to get to the next AI—and HackAPrompt—where you get AI’s to pass certain regulations, like not telling users how to carry out an assasination attempt on the whole UN (that is the beginner level fyi). I was able to pass three levels without any formal methods, just trail and error. This was two years ago, and there are now thousands of research papers of super easy cheat codes to get any AI to break. So, as prompt hacking is getting easier and more popular, it is more important for AI companies to ensure their models are as secure as possible, especially on their most confidential information.

A privacy related example of promptinghack this is the research done by Nicholas Carlini and his coauthors. In the paper “Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models,” they investigate whether information could be recovered from a language model after training. Through a series of queries, they successfully extracted hundreds of passages that it had reproduced almost exactly from its training data. Some of the recovered information included names, email addresses, phone numbers, online conversations, computer code, and unique identification numbers. And, some of the information only appeared once in the training data. Ultimately, they showed that no matter how small the data is the AI can memorize it and if the AI memorizes it, anyone online can discover it. This should be a big concern to users everywhere, because, as the saying goes “once something is online, it's there forever.” Furthermore, the research also showed that larger models were more vulnerable than smaller models to this attack. As the current trend in AI companies is to push out larger and larger models, this should be very concerning.

Even conversations you now have with AI can become training data. Last year, a Stanford study revealed that all six major U.S. AI companies it examined used users’ chatbot inputs to train or improve their models by default, with some retaining conversations indefinitely, combining them with information from other services, or allowing human reviewers to read chat transcripts. The researchers also found that privacy policies and opt-out options were often unclear, so users may not realize that sensitive messages or uploaded files can be reused for training. This expands the privacy problem beyond models leaking existing training data: ordinary chatbot conversations can introduce new confidential information that may later be memorized and exposed.

In total, because AI has become part of everyday life, protecting privacy is no longer only the responsibility of programmers or companies—it also requires users to think carefully about what they share. Ethically, developers must treat personal data as something entrusted to them, not simply as more information to improve a model.
